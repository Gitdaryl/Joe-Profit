# NEVER BROKEN: Why the Film Covers 1949 to 1971
*(Joe-facing notes. Written to be shared with Dr. Profit.)*

## The short version

Nothing from your life gets left out. It gets put where it wins.

A feature film is not a container, it is an arrow. It can carry one question the whole way through: can a boy born into a system built to break him make it out unbroken? The night your name came over that radio in 1971, and the day that university retired No. 40, the question is answered. Everything the audience needs to feel about the next fifty years, they feel in that room.

## Why we stop at the draft

1. **Movies run on struggle, not resume.** Audiences lean in during the work-boots season, the barn, the fights at NLU, the footlocker. They lean back during contract signings and award banquets. The business empire is astonishing on paper, but paper doesn't play. If we keep going past 1971, the most gripping film of the year slowly becomes a very good PowerPoint.

2. **The knee injury is more powerful as a card than as a scene.** When the film ends in triumph and the FIRST epilogue card says the career was over four games later, the whole theater gasps, and then the next six cards prove the title: football didn't make you, so losing it couldn't break you. Sixty seconds of title cards deliver the entire second half of the book at full emotional strength.

3. **One era, told deeply, beats six eras told thinly.** In two hours we can either live inside 1949 to 1971, or we can tour 1949 to 2015 from a bus. Every biopic that tried to cover everything (and there are many) is remembered for nothing. "42" covers roughly ONE season of Jackie Robinson's life. That focus is why it worked.

4. **The rest of the story is not cut. It is staged.** The plan has slots for all of it:
   - The film: 1949 to 1971.
   - The epilogue cards: business empire, Kuwait, MLK holiday, ministry, Hall of Fame, YUP.
   - The fundraise campaign and social content: interviews, archival footage, the real Joe today.
   - A future documentary or series: the boardroom years deserve their own telling, done right, not squeezed.

5. **Focus is also what raises the money.** Investors fund a movie they can describe in one breath. "Sharecropper's son breaks the color barrier and reaches the first round of the draft the season his father dies" is fundable. "The life story of a man who did many remarkable things across six decades" is not a pitch, it is a Wikipedia page.

## Where the "extras" go (and why that's good news)

Every piece of archival material, old interviews, clippings, photos, game film, is valuable, just not inside the drama. Real-world footage breaks the spell of a period film, but it is EXACTLY what the campaign around the film needs:

| Asset | Best home |
|---|---|
| Old interview footage (VHS-era) | Fundraise pitch video and epilogue montage: "the real Joe." After 90 seconds of cinematic trailer, cutting to the real man's face is the moment investors reach for the pen. |
| Photos, clippings, documents | Epilogue card backgrounds, pitch deck, social carousels |
| Book gallery (Chapter 18) | Poster and key art references, end-credit montage |
| Joe today, on camera | The final beat of the pitch video and the fundraise ask itself |

The test for the film is simple: does this scene push the 1949 to 1971 question forward? The test for the campaign is just as simple: is this real? Everything real goes in the campaign, where authenticity is the whole point.

## The one-sentence answer when something doesn't fit the film

"That's not a film scene, that's a campaign asset, and it's going to work harder there."
